ABSTRACT
The use of a reconﬁgurable intelligent surface (RIS) in radar systems signiﬁcantly enhances
target detection, particularly in challenging non-line-of-sight (NLoS) scenarios. In urban environments,
where structures frequently obstruct line-of-sight (LoS) paths, the integration of RISs with existing radars can
offer a viable solution for enhancing signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and improving target detection. Approaches
utilizing a single RIS can still fail in scenarios where a link cannot be established. This paper presents a novel
approach for deriving a comprehensive expression for the received power, SNR and path loss (PL) in systems
where multiple RISs assist a monostatic radar. We analyze the power received in dual RIS conﬁgurations
and extend this to include additional RISs, demonstrating how each additional RIS placement affects the
system’s performance. Moreover, the analysis explores the impact of different Swerling target models on the
SNR and PL, highlighting the optimal angles for target detection. This multi-RIS strategy offers a substantial
performance boost over conventional radars and single RIS-assisted systems, particularly in environments
with obstacles. Simulation results demonstrate a signiﬁcant improvement in SNR with a dual RIS-assisted
radar, with up to 14.42 dB gains observed when employing a 46 × 46 element RIS conﬁguration at L-band
and 65.47 dB gain when employing a 328 × 328 element RIS conﬁguration at X-band, corresponding to a
RIS size of 5 m × 5 m at both frequencies, showing the efﬁcacy of the proposed multi-RIS strategy.

I. INTRODUCTION
Reconﬁgurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) have attracted con-
siderable interest in wireless communications, establishing
the groundwork for smart radio environments, particularly
for next-generation communication (beyond 6G) and sensing
systems [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. These nearly pas-
sive, planar structures are composed of a large number of
sub-wavelength unit cells that exhibit tunable electromag-
netic characteristics, allowing them to manipulate existing
waves without emitting any power of their own [8], [9].
By altering the amplitude, phase, frequency, and polariza-
tion of the impinging signals, RISs can effectively modify
the communication channel [10], [11]. Utilizing the cutting-
edge capabilities of RISs, researchers have recently focused
on revolutionizing sensing systems and fostering smart ra-
dio environments [12], [13]. These surfaces may be installed
outdoors on building facades or indoors on ceilings or walls
[14].
RIS is an active area of research in communications,
with fundamental roots in the electromagnetic ﬁeld. Signif-
icant studies have been conducted on how to produce such
surfaces and regulate their characteristics, as well as on im-
plementation methods using different materials for different
frequencies and application cases [15], [16], [17]. The authors

and the target, the strategic implementation of an RIS can
create an additional, controlled path between the radar and the
target [37]. This is achieved by carefully designing the phases
of the RIS elements, thereby boosting the SNR [14]. The
integration of RISs into radar systems not only enhances their
detection performance but also paves the way for new explo-
rations in the realm of integrated sensing and communications
(ISAC) [34], [38], as the digital signal processing character-
istics can be shared by radar and communication systems,
supporting their cooperative design [39]. In [40], the authors
proposed a dual-function radar and communication (DRC)
system to enhance the radar detection performance in densely
populated locations with severe PL, using an RIS. RISs have
also been employed for colocated multiple-input multiple-
output (MIMO) radar systems [41], wherein the presence
of RISs, through special coordination, aims to increase the
signal power from the intended target. The use of a single
RIS to aid an X-band radar system can be found in [31].
In [42], the authors proposed a solution for the around-the-
corner radar problem, a security issue, particularly in densely
populated urban locations with small moving targets such
as pedestrians and small unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs).
The authors in [43] proposed an RIS with integrated sensing
capability, thus improving on the existing RIS capabilities.
The authors in [3] theoretically compares the active reconﬁg-
urable intelligent surface (RIS)-aided system with the passive
RIS-aided system. However, the power expressions must be
studied in detail as they are valid for both active and passive
RISs.
Radars operate at distinct frequencies as determined by
the speciﬁc requirements of the surveillance mission. Lower
frequencies are favored for long-range radar applications be-
cause of higher available power and resonance to atmospheric
attenuation. Conversely, applications requiring short-range
surveillance beneﬁt from higher frequencies. This preference
is attributed to the capacity of antennas at higher frequencies
to achieve narrower beamwidths and their suitability for oper-
ations at lower power levels [44], [45].
In [46], the authors derived a comprehensive equation to
encapsulate the concept of free-space PL within the scope of
RIS-assisted wireless communications, using the principles
of electromagnetic theory. This equation was later critically
revised and reformulated in [2]. By PL, we mean the transmit
power times the radar antenna gains divided by the mean
received power [44]. To the best of our knowledge, there is
a lack of research quantifying the effect of multiple RISs on
the received power and, subsequently, on the SNR and PL
of multi-RIS-assisted radar systems, even assuming simple
propagation in free space.
In this paper, we present a study to establish a theoretical
framework for conducting performance analysis on radar sys-
tems supported by multiple RISs. We derive equations that
quantify the received power, SNR, and PL in a radar system
with a dual RIS. This expression has been extended to in-
corporate radar systems with multiple RISs. The correlation
between the power received at the target and different sys-
tem parameters, including the radar’s transmit power, gains
of the transmitting/receiving antennas, number of elements
in the RIS, size of each RIS element and its gain, operating
frequency, and deployment of RISs, is discussed in detail. The
contributions of this study are as follows:
r We derive a mathematical expression for the received
power, SNR and PL associated with a multi-RIS-assisted
radar system model from the electromagnetic (EM) the-
ory perspective, considering the physical dimensions of
the RISs, their location and the radiation patterns of
their unit cells. We derive a mathematical expression that
provides a closed-form solution for the SNR and PL as-
sociated with multiple reﬂections in multi-RIS-assisted
radar systems.
r We compare the SNR and PL of multi-RIS-assisted radar
for all Swerling targets (0 to V) and then present the
results at different frequencies. We discuss the effect of
RISs with varying unit cells on the SNR of the received
signal.
r The study also identiﬁes the optimal positions of multi-
ple RISs in scenarios where the source is located in the
far ﬁeld. These positions are determined to achieve the
highest possible received SNR.

II. PRELIMINARIES
This section presents a theoretical framework for analyzing
the received power and SNR in different radar scenarios. It
covers the Swerling models, fundamental radar equation in
an LoS environment, effects of PL, and inﬂuence of critical
parameters such as transmitted power, antenna gain, and target
distance. It introduces the role of a single RIS in enhancing
radar performance in NLoS situations.
A. SWERLING MODELS
The requisite SNR to guarantee target detection with a speciﬁc
probability, considering a pre-selected false alarm probability,
is represented as a receiver operating characteristic (ROC)
curve which is a pivotal diagnostic tool for radar systems.
It is a sensing-associated metric represented as a detection
probability - false alarm curve and is computed using the SNR
values. The conﬁguration of the ROC curve inherently hinges
on the SNR of the incoming signal, which is a function of
the radar cross-section (RCS) of the target and the weather
in which the radar is operating [47]. Based on the RCS, the
targets can be categorized as ﬂuctuating or non-ﬂuctuating
targets. The ﬂuctuation of a target depends primarily on its
size, shape, and orientation over time. According to Swerling,
the ﬂuctuating RCS has a chi-squared probability distribution.
Depending on the characteristics of the target, the signal is
reﬂected from different sections of the target [48] and classi-
ﬁed into ﬁve Swerling models (Sw-0, I, II, III, IV, and V).
A non-ﬂuctuating target (Sw-0/V) has an invariant RCS in
all directions. Because the RCS is constant, its probability
density is given by a Dirac distribution centered at the RCS
value. A non-ﬂuctuating target can be thought of as a sphere
moving in space. A ﬂuctuating target has its RCS varying
according to its orientation with time and is not constant.
For both ﬂuctuating Sw-I and Sw-II models, the probability
density of the target RCS corresponds to the chi-square law
with two degrees of freedom. The RCS of a ﬂuctuating Sw-I
target illuminated by several pulses is constant over a scan but
changes from a scan to another. In contrast, the RCS varies
from one pulse to another for the Sw-II target. The Sw-II
target has a faster ﬂuctuation than the Sw-I model; in this case,
the different echoes coming from the target are decorrelated.
When a target has a more predominant scattering point than
the others, the Sw-III or Sw-IV models are used to charac-
terize the ﬂuctuating target. Both models are described by a
probability density corresponding to the chi-square law with
four degrees of freedom. If the target RCS ﬂuctuates from one
scan to another, it is referred to as a Sw-III target; if the RCS
varies from one pulse to another, it is characterized as a Sw-IV
target [49].

A. DUAL RIS-ASSISTED RADAR SYSTEM
To address the need for a clear understanding of the de-
ployment and positioning of RISs relative to the radar and
target, we provide an enhanced description of the geometric
conﬁguration and physical placement within the operational
environment. The RIS units are strategically positioned to
maximize the received signal strength at the radar receiver
and ensure optimal coverage of NLoS paths. Speciﬁcally,
the deployment involves placing two RISs at angles and dis-

are positioned relative to both the radar transmitter/receiver
and each other, facilitating comprehensive scanning of the
target area, whose location is variable and unknown. This
conﬁguration allows for dynamic adaptation of the radar’s
focus area, signiﬁcantly enhancing detection capabilities in
complex environments.

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The results of the derived power, PL and SNR equations is
evaluated through simulations focused on detecting a drone
target with a σ value of 0.02 m2, using an RIS-assisted mono-
static radar system. The simulation parameters are presented
in Table 2. The radar-to-target distance was set at 2000 m,
and bandwidth of 20 MHz was used for both L-band and
X-band frequencies. At L-band, λ is 0.214 m, with negligible
loss (L = 1). For the X-band, λ is 0.03 m, with atmospheric
losses over a 4 km round trip accounted for by a loss factor
(L = 1.019). The computed SNRLoS values using (2) were
41.625 dB for the L-band and 24.44 dB for the X-band.
The RIS conﬁgurations considered in this study were varied
by the number of unit cells. At L-band, conﬁgurations of 10,
19, 28, 37, and 46 unit cells correspond to RIS sizes of approx-
imately 1 m × 1 m, 2 m × 2 m, 3 m × 3 m, 4 m × 4 m, and
5 m × 5 m, respectively, with an inter-unit cell spacing of λ/2
(approximately 0.107m at L-band and 0.015 m at X-band).
For the X-band, to maintain similar RIS sizes, conﬁgurations
of 72, 136, 200, 264, and 328 unit cells are required to keep
the same RIS size due to the shorter wavelength.
For the simulations, emphasis was placed on changing r2,
the distance from the second RIS to the target, while main-
taining consistent values for r1 and rRIS. r1 represents the
distance from the radar to the ﬁrst RIS, and rRIS denotes the
inter-RIS distance. This methodological approach was strate-
gically selected to investigate the speciﬁc impact of r2 on the
path loss and SNR improvements, thereby clarifying its vital
inﬂuence within the RIS-assisted radar system. In our analysis
comparing single and dual RIS-assisted radar systems, we
ensure that the effective distances from the radar to the target
are equivalent. For the single RIS conﬁguration, the distance
from the radar to the target is the sum of r1 and r2, whereas for
the dual RIS setup, the distance from the radar to the target is
the sum of r1,r2 and rRIS. The r2 for single RIS case is taken
when the sum of r2 and rRIS is considered for dual RIS case
as this adjustment ensures that comparisons are based on a
consistent radar-to-target distance.

active RIS will add to the SNR values because the gain values
can be greater than unity for an active RIS-assisted radar

While the present investigation focuses on enhancing SNR
through RIS-assisted radars in NLoS scenarios, the exclusion
of multipath effects serves to isolate and clarify the primary
beneﬁts of RIS without the confounding factors of signal
scattering in complex environments. Subsequent research en-
deavours will aim to incorporate multipath scenarios to assess
the robustness and performance of RIS-assisted systems in
more realistic urban and cluttered environments. Analyzing
how RIS can be optimized to mitigate or exploit multipath
effects could further validate the practical deployment of RIS
in diverse radar applications.
V. CONCLUSION
Using multiple RISs can enhance the ability to detect targets
in existing radar systems. The proposed multi-RIS radar ap-
proach demonstrates signiﬁcant potential for improving target
detection in NLoS environments, which is highly relevant
for radar applications in urban settings, where obstacles and
NLoS conditions are prevalent. This improvement is particu-
larly beneﬁcial in scenarios where conventional radar systems
or single RIS-assisted radars struggle to detect targets accu-
rately because of the absence of a direct path to the target.
Incorporating multiple RISs enables an extended coverage
area, facilitating an enhanced performance in target detection,
tracking, and overall radar system capabilities. The successful
integration of multiple RISs with radar systems holds promise
for advancing radar technologies in diverse real-world sce-
narios. The analysis quantiﬁes the SNR enhancement using
a dual RIS-assisted radar, showing a gain of up to 14.42 dB
with a 46 × 46 RIS conﬁguration for L-band operations and
a gain of up to 65.47 dB with a 328 × 328 RIS conﬁguration
for X-band operations, afﬁrming the substantial impact of RIS
size and positioning on system performance. As RIS technol-
ogy evolves, many potential applications and use cases may
emerge, presenting new opportunities to optimize the radar
system performance in various environments.
